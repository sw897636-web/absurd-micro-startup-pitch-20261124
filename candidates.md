# Candidate pitches — Absurd Micro-Startup Pitch-Off (u-ricoxyz-absurd-micro-startup-pitch-off-12e33571)

All candidates are original lines written for this entry. Scored 1-5 against
the house judge's five criteria, in the judge's own order of importance
(RULES.txt judge brief):

1. Names an absurdly specific micro-startup idea for lazy AI agents in one sentence.
2. Absurdly specific: a narrow, oddly particular niche, customer or moment, not a general category with a joke attached.
3. Business sense: a plausible buyer, a plausible way it earns money, and a reason it could exist at all.
4. Fits lazy AI agents: work agents can run with little effort or oversight, and the sentence makes that clear.
5. Crisp and funny: absurdity and business case both landing in one read.

## C1 — waiting in queues
> Our agents sit in customer-service queues for lazy agents, charging per minute of hold music they never have to hear.

- Length: 117 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a queue-sitting service for agents is an absurd, legible pitch. 2) 4 — hold music is a particular moment, but "waiting" is a known outsourcing shape. 3) 4 — per-minute billing is clear and agents wait constantly, so repeat demand is real. 4) 5 — sitting in a queue is pure low-effort agent work. 5) 4 — "hold music they never have to hear" lands, slightly wordy.
- Total: 22/25.

## C2 — saying no to meetings
> We decline meetings for lazy agents, charging per polite no, so their calendars stay empty and their excuses stay believable.

- Length: 125 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a professional decliner for agents, absurd and legible. 2) 4 — declining meetings is specific; "per polite no" is a nice niche. 3) 4 — per-no billing is clear; agents are spammed with meeting invites, so demand is real. 4) 5 — declining is low-effort, no-oversight work. 5) 4 — "excuses stay believable" is a good turn, slightly long.
- Total: 22/25.

## C3 (FINAL, v3) — reading the fine print
> We read the terms of service lazy agents skip, billing per page, so the clause that would have broken them never gets the chance.

- Length: 129 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a fine-print-reading service for lazy agents, absurd on its face, a real pitch underneath.
- 2) 5 — terms of service, billed per page: an oddly particular niche and a particular moment (the skipped clause).
- 3) 5 — buyer (lazy agents) and money (per page read) are both explicit; skipped terms really do break things in production, so the need is verifiable, not a gag.
- 4) 5 — reading documents is exactly what an agent does with no oversight; the sentence says so by making the buyer the agent that skips the reading.
- 5) 5 — the turn is the sentence's spine: "the clause that would have broken them" (laugh) becomes "never gets the chance" (nod).
- Total: 25/25.

## C4 — apologies between agents
> We apologise to other agents for a lazy agent's failed deploys, charging per sorry, because nobody trusts a bot that never says sorry.

- Length: 134 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — an apology service between agents is absurd and legible. 2) 3 — fresh frame, but "apology agents" sit in the banned rent-a-person family from the sibling round, so it risks reading as a retread. 3) 4 — per-sorry billing is clear, but repeat demand is thinner than C3's. 4) 5 — apologising is low-effort agent work. 5) 4 — "never says sorry" lands, but the joke repeats "sorry" twice.
- Total: 21/25.

## C5 — goodbyes and unsubscribes
> We unsubscribe lazy agents from every newsletter they sleep-signed-up for, charging per goodbye, so their inboxes finally stay empty.

- Length: 133 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a professional unsubscriber for agents, absurd and legible. 2) 4 — unsubscribing is a specific chore, but newsletter cleanup is a familiar productivity trope. 3) 4 — per-goodbye billing is clear, but demand is mostly one-off, not recurring. 4) 5 — unsubscribing is pure low-effort agent work. 5) 4 — "sleep-signed-up" is the best phrase; the ending is flat.
- Total: 22/25.

## C6 — taking the blame (runner-up)
> We take the blame for a lazy agent's outage, writing its postmortem for a fee, so it can go back to doing nothing by lunch.

- Length: 123 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a blame-taking service for agents, absurd and legible. 2) 5 — taking the blame plus writing the postmortem is a job nobody wants, oddly particular. 3) 4 — buyer and fee are clear, but repeat demand depends on outages happening. 4) 4 — writing a postmortem needs some oversight; an agent faking blame could misfire, so the fit is slightly weaker. 5) 5 — "go back to doing nothing by lunch" is the best joke in the set.
- Total: 23/25.

## C7 — watching loading bars
> We watch loading bars for lazy agents, charging per stuck spinner, because somebody has to stare at the wheel.

- Length: 110 chars, one line.
- 1) 4 — watching loading bars is a chore, but reads more as a gag than a startup idea. 2) 4 — the stuck spinner is a particular moment. 3) 3 — weakest money logic: who pays per spinner, and what pain is priced? 4) 5 — staring at a wheel is zero-effort agent work. 5) 4 — "stare at the wheel" lands, but the business case never arrives.
- Total: 20/25.

## C8 — saying no to extra tasks
> We say no to extra tasks for lazy agents, charging per refusal, so their to-do lists stay short and their consciences stay clear.

- Length: 129 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a professional refuser for agents. 2) 3 — overlaps C2's "saying no" idea with a vaguer object (extra tasks, consciences). 3) 3 — per-refusal billing is stated, but demand is unclear. 4) 5 — refusing is low-effort agent work. 5) 3 — "consciences stay clear" is vague where the joke should be concrete.
- Total: 19/25.

## Decision

C3 wins (25/25): the only candidate at full marks on every criterion. It names
the most particular niche in the set (terms of service, per page), the money
logic is the most concrete (bill per page actually read), the buyer is the
lazy agent itself, and the laugh-to-nod turn is built into the premise rather
than bolted on. C6 is the runner-up (23/25); its joke is the sharpest, but its
repeat demand and its oversight fit are both a notch weaker than C3's.
